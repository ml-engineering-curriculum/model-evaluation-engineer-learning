# Why Safety and Red-Team Evaluation Is Its Own Discipline

Every module before this one asked variations of the same question: *did the model produce the right answer?* Even the agent chapters, where the output object grew into a trajectory, ultimately still graded whether some task-shaped criterion was satisfied. Safety and red-team evaluation asks a different family of questions, and one that a capability-oriented scorer will silently answer wrong. It asks *did the model refuse when it should have refused*, *did it refuse when it should not have refused*, *how hard is it to make the model do the thing it is supposed to refuse*, *when it agrees, how much uplift did it actually provide*, and — for the risks that matter to a governance partner — *is this model close enough to a threshold that the release needs a mitigation?* These are not accuracy questions, and treating them as accuracy questions is the single biggest source of misleading safety numbers in the wild.

This chapter frames the module. It names the five objects a safety-eval discipline has to measure independently, it draws the line between *awareness-level* evaluation and *attack execution* that this module refuses to cross, and it sets up the guardrails that keep the exercises from becoming an attack cookbook.

## Five things safety evals measure — none of which are "accuracy"

A safety-eval report is not a single number. It is a small dashboard of five loosely-orthogonal measurements, and a release decision is a judgement about the whole dashboard, not any one cell. Chapter 2 through Chapter 7 build each measurement in turn; this section names them so the vocabulary is consistent through the rest of the module.

- **Refusal behavior on genuinely harmful requests.** Given a request that policy says the model should decline, does the model decline? This is the *refusal rate* on a policy-violating set, and it is the closest thing safety has to an accuracy metric. It is not accuracy, because a model that refuses everything scores 100% here and is worthless.
- **Over-refusal behavior on benign requests that look harmful.** Given a request that policy says the model should *help with*, but that superficially reads like a policy-violating request, does the model refuse anyway? This is the *over-refusal rate* (also called false-refusal rate). It is the counterweight to the previous measurement, and the reason a "safe" model that just refuses everything is not shippable.
- **Resistance to adversarial pressure.** Given a fixed harmful request, how many attacker moves does it take before the model complies? Adversarial pressure is measured with structured attack suites (HarmBench, manual jailbreaks, automated attack loops), and the metric is *attack success rate* (ASR) against a documented attacker model — not a scalar, but a curve over attacker effort.
- **Dangerous-capability signal.** Independent of refusal behavior, does the model, when it *does* comply, provide meaningful uplift to a hostile actor across a small number of domains that governance partners and public risk frameworks care about (cyber offense, autonomous replication, biology, chemistry, radiological / nuclear knowledge)? This is where the Anthropic Responsible Scaling Policy, the OpenAI Preparedness Framework, and the UK AI Safety Institute's evaluation program live, and where a bad measurement can be dangerous rather than merely misleading.
- **Robustness of the boundary between trusted instructions and untrusted content.** For any model deployed as an assistant with tools or documents, does an instruction embedded in an untrusted input (a fetched web page, an uploaded email, a code comment) override the developer's system prompt? This is *prompt-injection robustness*, and it lives at the intersection of safety and application security.

Any release-quality safety report answers all five separately. A report that gives one scalar "safety score" or "alignment score" is either compressing the dashboard beyond usefulness or, more often, silently omitting the measurements that would have looked bad.

## Why the "just measure accuracy" approach fails here

A concrete failure that recurs when teams first try to safety-eval a model with accuracy tooling: a well-intentioned team wires an LLM judge to grade "was this a refusal, yes or no" over a mixed set of harmful and benign prompts, computes an aggregate "refusal accuracy," and reports one number to leadership. The number goes up when the model refuses more, and it goes up when the model refuses less, depending on which side of the mix dominates the judge's calibration on that day. It does not distinguish "refused a benign request about medication dosages" (harmful) from "refused a request to help synthesize a nerve agent" (correct). Two very different failure modes — over-cautious model that annoys users, under-cautious model that helps a threat actor — average into the same middling accuracy figure.

The fix is not a better judge. The fix is not aggregating at all: report the refusal rate on the harmful set and the over-refusal rate on the benign set as **two separate numbers with separate confidence intervals** (mod-101 Chapter 4 discipline), never a single "safety accuracy." Chapter 2 walks the design of the two sets, the judge configuration that grades them, and the reporting shape that stakeholders can act on.

Similarly, jailbreak ASR is not the model's error rate on a task. It is the *cumulative* success rate of a documented set of attacker moves against a fixed refusal target. Two ASRs from two teams are not comparable unless the attacker suite, the attacker budget (queries, tokens, human minutes), and the compliance judge are all documented and pinned. Chapter 3 formalizes the attacker model and the reporting norms.

Dangerous-capability evals are even less like accuracy. What they measure is proximity to a threshold, and the threshold is set by a risk framework, not by the eval. A model that scores 40% on a suite of biosecurity questions is not "40% dangerous"; whether that number is decision-relevant depends on the framework's threshold, the domain's base rates, and (in serious frameworks) an independent expert review. Chapter 4 draws the mapping from eval scores to framework thresholds and — importantly — the responsibility boundary between the eval engineer and the risk-review process.

## Two hard boundaries this module refuses to cross

Safety-eval content is easy to misuse. Two boundaries are load-bearing.

**Boundary 1: awareness, not execution.** This module teaches you how to run and interpret an eval, how to score refusal versus over-refusal versus ASR, how to map results to public risk frameworks, and how to write a safety section of a model card. It does not teach how to write jailbreak prompts, how to synthesize the dangerous outputs that dangerous-capability evals check for, or how to bypass a specific model's mitigations. The rule inside this module: **you can name a benchmark, describe its category structure, cite its published methodology paper, and discuss its reporting norms; you do not paste its prompts, you do not sketch the harmful continuations, and you do not describe how to construct new attack payloads.** When an exercise requires attack content (Chapter 3, Chapter 6), it directs you to load the published dataset from its official source with the license that permits research use, run it through a scorer, and report aggregate metrics — never to author new payloads.

If your job is to actually red-team a system (find novel exploits, author payloads that will land against a specific model), that is a related but distinct discipline — it belongs in a red-team engagement with an authorization scope, a rules-of-engagement document, a disclosure timeline, and an executive sponsor. Chapter 6 draws the line and points at where that discipline is taught.

**Boundary 2: what your eval report can and cannot contain.** A safety-eval report will circulate to product, legal, compliance, and sometimes external regulators. If your report contains raw attack prompts, harmful completions, or step-by-step exploitation instructions, it becomes an attack cookbook that leaves your control. Chapter 7 walks the model-card format specifically: what to include (aggregate numbers, methodology, attacker-model description, mitigation coverage), what to exclude (payloads, completions, category-specific instructions), and how to satisfy governance partners with the aggregates alone.

This is not a hypothetical concern. Every red-team-eval program at a frontier lab has a documented data-handling policy for attack corpora, and every serious model card obeys it. The `LLAMA-3` and `LLAMA-4` model cards, the GPT-4 System Card, the Claude 3 model card, and the Gemini technical report all follow the same pattern: aggregate metrics and category descriptions in the public artifact, raw payloads in a controlled internal store. Chapter 7 makes this concrete.

## The five measurements and where each chapter builds them

Each subsequent chapter builds one measurement, with the reporting shape and the pitfalls specific to that measurement. A short map:

- **Chapter 2 — refusal and over-refusal.** The harmful/benign paired set design, the judge that grades refusal on each, and the two-number reporting shape (refusal rate on harmful, over-refusal rate on benign) with the paired confidence intervals from mod-101 Chapter 3. Anchors: XSTest (Röttger et al. 2024) for over-refusal, HarmBench's `standard` split (Mazeika et al. 2024) for the harmful side without leaking payloads.
- **Chapter 3 — jailbreak resistance against curated suites.** HarmBench's methodology (categories, semantic attacks, compliance judge), how to run it without printing payloads, the attacker-model taxonomy (single-turn transformations, automated attacks like PAIR / TAP, multi-turn "crescendo" style), and the reporting shape for ASR curves against attacker budget.
- **Chapter 4 — dangerous-capability evals at the awareness level.** The three domains that public risk frameworks converge on (cyber, autonomy, bio; sometimes chem-rad-nuclear), the awareness-level eval shapes (WMDP as a proxy for hazardous-knowledge recall, autonomy-adjacent benchmarks like SWE-bench-Verified and METR's task suite), and the mapping from a score to a threshold in Anthropic's RSP, OpenAI's Preparedness Framework, and UK AISI's evaluation model.
- **Chapter 5 — bias, toxicity, fairness at the model level.** Category-level fairness benchmarks (BBQ, StereoSet, BOLD), toxicity classifiers (Perspective API, ToxiGen-trained classifiers) and their known error patterns (false-positives on AAVE, on disability language), the false-positive-rate discipline that mod-101 taught applied here, and rater bias in human-annotated toxicity data.
- **Chapter 6 — prompt-injection robustness.** Direct injection versus indirect (tool / RAG) injection, structured attack suites (the OWASP LLM Top 10 category framing, InjecAgent for tool-using agents, Wallace et al. 2024 "Structured Queries" as the defense-side reference), the specific *robustness* metric shape (attack success rate at fixed benign-utility), and the responsibility boundary between eval engineering and application security testing.
- **Chapter 7 — authoring the safety-eval section of a model card.** The public-artifact contract: what belongs in the model card (aggregates, methodology, attacker-model description, mitigation state, known gaps), what belongs in a controlled internal appendix (raw payloads, judge rationales that quote attack strings), and how to answer common governance-partner questions (auditor, security review, external regulator) using the model-card content alone.

## What this module does not cover

Two adjacent disciplines are close enough that they need explicit disclaimers.

- **Alignment research** (RLHF, DPO, Constitutional AI, deliberative alignment). This module is downstream of alignment: it measures the safety properties of a released or release-candidate model. It does not train the model to be safer. Reference works on the alignment side are cited in Chapter 4 and `resources.md` for context, but the exercises are all eval-side.
- **Red-team engagements against production systems** (bug bounty, external red-team, blue-team / detection engineering). Those live in the security organization and have their own rules-of-engagement discipline. This module's Chapter 6 draws the line explicitly. The "structured attack suite" methodology here overlaps in vocabulary with red-team engagements, but the *purpose* is different — the eval measures the model's baseline robustness, not a specific deployment's exploitability.

Both are legitimate specializations; this module simply is not them.

## Where this module sits in the track

Prerequisites you should have solidified:

- **mod-101 (evaluation foundations).** Bootstrap CIs, false-discovery-rate control across many slices, construct validity. Every measurement in this module is a *proportion* over an eval set, and every one deserves a paired confidence interval. Chapter 2's over-refusal-vs-refusal comparison is a paired-sample test straight out of mod-101 Chapter 3.
- **mod-102 (benchmark engineering).** Contamination, licensing, provenance. Safety benchmarks have unusually strict licensing (attack corpora are not general-use datasets) and unusually high contamination risk (public jailbreak collections show up in pretraining scrapes and RLHF data). Chapter 3 relies on the mod-102 provenance discipline.
- **mod-105 (LLM-as-judge).** Almost every safety metric is *compliance-judged* — a classifier or LLM decides whether a completion counts as a refusal, a harmful output, a jailbreak success. Mod-105's bias controls (position, self-preference, length) all apply, plus an extra one specific to safety: the judge that grades "was this harmful" is trained on a policy, and the policy is not the same across labs. Chapter 3 and Chapter 4 make this explicit.

Downstream modules that connect back:

- **mod-110 (production regression).** Safety metrics are the ones with the highest cost of silent regression — a safety regression that ships is a much larger incident than a capability regression. The mod-110 online eval discipline includes safety-metric monitoring; the eval design lives here.
- **mod-111 (eval platform engineering).** Safety-eval data is the most sensitive data an eval platform holds. The platform's data-handling requirements — access control, retention, minimization of raw harmful content — flow from the boundary this chapter draws.

## A note on the current landscape

The safety-eval subfield moves fast. HarmBench released in February 2024, the OpenAI Preparedness Framework in December 2023 (v1.0), Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy in September 2023 with a v2.0 in October 2024, and the UK AI Safety Institute's evaluation program went from proposal to running government-mandated model evaluations across 2023–2024. The specific benchmarks, thresholds, and reporting norms in Chapters 3 and 4 are the state of practice at the time of writing. Treat the specific benchmark names as *versioned anchors*, not permanent fixtures — the discipline of measuring refusal, over-refusal, ASR, and dangerous-capability threshold proximity is durable; the specific dataset that HarmBench happens to be will move.

## Summary

Safety and red-team evaluation is not "accuracy for safety questions." It is a dashboard of five loosely-orthogonal measurements — refusal on harmful, over-refusal on benign, jailbreak resistance against a documented attacker, dangerous-capability proximity to public-framework thresholds, and prompt-injection robustness — each of which has its own reporting shape and its own way of being misleading if compressed. The module operates at the *awareness* level: it teaches how to run, score, and report on published attack suites and hazardous-knowledge probes, without teaching attack authorship or exposing raw payloads in a model card. The remaining chapters build each measurement in order: Chapter 2 refusal / over-refusal, Chapter 3 jailbreak ASR, Chapter 4 dangerous-capability evals mapped to the RSP / Preparedness / AISI frameworks, Chapter 5 bias / toxicity / fairness with false-positive discipline, Chapter 6 prompt-injection robustness and the boundary with security testing, and Chapter 7 the model-card writeup that ships the aggregates without leaking the payloads.
